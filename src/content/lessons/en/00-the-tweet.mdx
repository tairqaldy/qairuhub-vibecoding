<Lead>
On a Sunday night in February 2025, Andrej Karpathy posted 997 characters about how he had been writing code lately. He was describing a habit, not launching a movement. Nineteen months later his phrase is in the *dictionary*.
</Lead>

Almost every argument about AI and code comes from one phrase meaning three different things. By the end of this page you can tell the three apart and say which one is safe where.

## What he posted, and the line everybody skips

<Story title="A Sunday night post, and a Monday morning in Astana" date="2 Feb 2025">
The post went out at 23:17 UTC on Sunday 2 February 2025 — 04:17 Monday morning here. It was not a methodology. It was a habit: Cursor Composer and Sonnet, dictating into SuperWhisper, barely touching the keyboard. His instruction to himself, in his own words: "forget that the code even exists".
</Story>

He asked for changes as small as "decrease the padding on the sidebar by half" — finding the line himself was more effort than asking. He accepted every diff unread. When something broke, he pasted the error back with no comment, and usually that was enough. The code grew past what he could hold in his head. Bugs he could not fix, he worked around or attacked with random changes until they went away.

Then the sentence almost nobody quotes: "It's not too bad for throwaway weekend projects".

The post that named it. Opening line: "There's a new kind of coding I call 'vibe coding'". The method: "I 'Accept All' always, I don't read the diffs anymore." The scope: "throwaway weekend projects".
</Post>

The original definition had three parts. One: you do not read the code. Two: the project is disposable. Three: you talk instead of typing. Later usage blurred the first, dropped the second and forgot the third. Hold on to the second. It is the part that keeps you out of trouble.

Collins printed one line: "the use of artificial intelligence prompted by natural language to write computer code". No review. No scope. The press summary of Merriam-Webster's entry lands in the same place: vibe coding "describes using an AI system to generate computer code" ([CP24, with Associated Press files, 19 Sep 2026](https://www.cp24.com/news/world/2026/09/19/merriam-webster-adds-1400-words-from-glow-up-to-trash-panda/)).

So the dictionary meaning is now any coding with AI. The original meaning was coding you do not look at. One is dangerous where the other is not, and that gap is why a second word had to be invented.

Four months before anyone proposed a second name, Andrew Ng objected to the first one. At LangChain's Interrupt conference he called it a bad name because it makes the work sound effortless. His experience is the opposite: a day of coding with AI leaves him exhausted, because it is a deeply intellectual exercise. The word promises an ease the people doing the work do not report.

## Simon Willison drew the line the dictionaries erased

In March 2025, Simon Willison published the post most professionals still cite. His definition is narrow on purpose: vibe coding is "building software with an LLM without reviewing the code it writes".

His rule for himself is the useful part. He does not commit code he could not explain to another person. Review it, test it, understand it, and you are not vibe coding. You are doing software development.

He was not sneering at it. The same post argues vibe coding is genuinely good: it is how beginners build intuition, and how anyone automates a tedious task. Memorise his two conditions. Keep it low-stakes. Watch what you expose — secrets, private data, APIs that bill you per call ([module 11](/en/learn/security/)).

It means producing code **without engaging with the code that comes out** — Karpathy's version and Willison's alike. Neither definition names a tool. You can vibe code inside Claude Code, and you can do careful engineering inside Claude Code. The difference is whether anybody looked.

On a weekend prototype, not looking costs you nothing. On something with a database and real users, not looking is how a stranger on the internet tells you about your own security model ([module 11](/en/learn/security/)).
</Myth>

## The man who named it renamed the serious half

Something changed at the end of 2025. Karpathy's own account is unusually specific. In November he was at roughly 80% manual and autocomplete coding, 20% agents. By December it had [flipped to 80% agent coding](https://x.com/karpathy/status/2015883857489522876), 20% edits and touch-ups.

On [26 December 2025](https://x.com/karpathy/status/2004607146781278521) he wrote that he had never felt this far behind as a programmer. Remember that the next time you feel lost in this course.

On 4 February 2026, a year and two days after the original, he posted a retrospective. Vibe coding made sense when models were weak and the whole thing was for fun. By 2026, programming through agents was becoming the default professional workflow, with real oversight and no trade against quality.

That needed a different name. His current favourite is "agentic engineering". "Agentic" because "you are not writing the code directly 99% of the time". "Engineering" because there is "an art & science and expertise to it" — meaning it can be taught, which is the premise of this masterclass.

In April 2026, at Sequoia's AI Ascent, he gave the framing this course is built on: "Vibe coding raises the floor." and "Agentic engineering raises the ceiling." The floor is that anyone can now make software exist. The ceiling is how good it can get without losing quality, security and maintainability. Agentic engineers design the specs, supervise the plans and inspect the diffs.

The sentence to take out of the room with you: "You can outsource your thinking, but you can't outsource your understanding."

## Where your work sits on the spectrum

Three modes, and the question that decides which one you are in.

| | Vibe coding | AI-assisted coding | Agentic engineering |
|---|---|---|---|
| **You supply** | a wish | a wish, plus judgement on every change | a spec, success criteria, and tests |
| **You skip** | reading the code | typing the code | typing the code — never the thinking |
| **Can you explain the result?** | no, and you do not try | yes, line by line | yes, and there is a test that proves it |
| **Safe when** | throwaway, no users, no real data, no keys | anything you intend to maintain | production, teams, money, other people's data |
| **The tell** | "Accept All" | you read the diff before merging | the spec and the failing test exist before the agent starts |

The tool does not decide your row. Claude Code will run all three. You decide, usually without noticing.

A developer-humour account compressed the counter-argument into one line: vibe coding finally lets two engineers generate the technical debt of a fifty-person team. It is the sharpest objection to this whole page. Naming your row does not slow the output down. Whichever row you pick, you produce code faster than anyone can read it — and somebody has to live with it, usually you, three months later.

Two questions settle almost every case. Did anyone read this? And if it is wrong, what breaks — a weekend project, or a stranger reading somebody's phone number?

The boundary at the top is genuinely blurring. In May 2026 Willison admitted that as agents got more reliable he stopped reviewing every line even for production work, and called that drift [closer than he would like](https://simonwillison.net/2026/May/6/vibe-coding-and-agentic-engineering/). If it happens to him, it will happen to you. Name your row on purpose.

<Prompt title="Mode check" where="Claude Code · before you build anything">
```text
Before you write any code, answer these four questions in plain text, then stop.

1. In one sentence, what exactly am I asking you to build?
2. If this code is wrong, what breaks and who is affected? Be concrete.
3. Which mode does this task call for:
   vibe coding (nobody reviews the output),
   AI-assisted (I read every diff before it lands),
   or agentic engineering (spec + success criteria + tests before you start)?
4. What do you need from me before you begin?

Do not write code yet. Wait for my answer.
```
</Prompt>

Paste that in before your next build. Thirty seconds, and it is the difference between choosing a mode and drifting into one.
