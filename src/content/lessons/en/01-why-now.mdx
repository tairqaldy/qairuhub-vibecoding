## The 95% number is real, and it is not what you think

In March 2025 Jared Friedman, a managing partner at Y Combinator, said that for about a quarter of the startups in YC's Winter 2025 batch, roughly 95% of the codebase was AI-generated ([TechCrunch, 6 March 2025](https://techcrunch.com/2025/03/06/a-quarter-of-startups-in-ycs-current-cohort-have-codebases-that-are-almost-entirely-ai-generated/)).

Read it again. It is 25% of one batch, not 95% of Y Combinator. The 95% counts human-typed characters against AI-typed ones, and leaves out imported library code.

Friedman's framing never travelled: those founders could have written every line by hand. In the same video Diana Hu said judging model output takes taste and training, and Garry Tan said the reasoning models of the day were poor at debugging.

Experienced builders with a good tool stopped typing. That does not mean typing was the hard part.

<Deeper title="Why four honest answers to the same question all disagree">
Ask "how much code does AI write?" and you get four numbers. All defensible, all measuring different things.

- **Friedman, March 2025:** ~95% of the codebase, for ~25% of one YC batch, imports excluded.
- **Sundar Pichai, 22 April 2026:** 75% of all new code at Google, in his words "AI-generated and approved by engineers" ([blog.google](https://blog.google/innovation-and-ai/infrastructure-and-cloud/google-cloud/cloud-next-2026-sundar-pichai/)). Google's metric changed meaning along the way, from autocomplete characters to agent output. Semafor called this class of claim "pretty impossible to fact-check" ([24 April 2026](https://www.semafor.com/article/04/24/2026/google-ceo-says-75-of-companys-new-code-is-ai-generated)).
- **Satya Nadella, April 2025:** 20–30% of code in Microsoft repos, better in Python than in C++ ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2025/04/29/microsoft-ceo-says-up-to-30-of-the-companys-code-was-written-by-ai/)).
- **Independent measurement:** about 29% of new Python functions in the US were AI-written by the end of 2024, up from 5% in 2022 — a classifier run over 30 million contributions from roughly 160,000 GitHub developers, published in *Science* ([Complexity Science Hub](https://csh.ac.at/news/ai-is-already-writing-almost-one-third-of-new-software-code/)).

Four denominators. When someone quotes one at you, the question is not "is that true?" but "of what?"
</Deeper>

## The best trial we have said the opposite

METR evaluates AI systems. In early 2025 it ran the trial everyone now argues about, published on [10 July 2025](https://metr.org/blog/2025-07-10-early-2025-ai-experienced-os-dev-study/).

<Evidence
  src="/img/evidence/metr-slowdown.png"
  alt="METR’s July 2025 study page, showing a banner that says the results are out of date"
  href="https://metr.org/blog/2025-07-10-early-2025-ai-experienced-os-dev-study/"
  source="METR"
  date="10 Jul 2025"
>
The yellow banner is METR's own. Anyone quoting the 19% without it is quoting half a study.
</Evidence>

Sixteen experienced open-source developers. 246 real issues from their own repositories — mature projects averaging over a million lines of code, five years of work each. Every issue was randomly assigned to AI-allowed or AI-disallowed. Mostly Cursor Pro with Claude 3.5 and 3.7 Sonnet.

With AI, the tasks took **19% longer**.

Before starting, those developers expected to be 24% faster. After finishing every task, they still believed they had been about 20% faster. The stopwatch said 19% slower. That is a 40-point gap between feeling and measurement, and the people inside it were experts on their own code.

<Deeper title="What the METR study does not say">
METR was unusually clear about the limits. The result says nothing about most developers, juniors, unfamiliar codebases or future models, and it is not evidence that better ways of using AI do not exist.

The setting was the hardest case for AI on purpose: experts, enormous mature repositories, a high quality bar, and years of context that never reaches the agent ([module 06](/en/learn/context-ssot/)). "Studies prove AI makes developers slower" misquotes a study whose own authors refuse that sentence.
</Deeper>

## What happened to that result afterwards

On [24 February 2026](https://metr.org/blog/2026-02-24-uplift-update/) METR published the rerun: 57 developers (10 returning, 47 new), more than 800 tasks across 143 repositories, starting in August 2025.

<Evidence
  src="/img/evidence/metr-update-2026.png"
  alt="METR’s February 2026 follow-up study with newer data"
  href="https://metr.org/blog/2026-02-24-uplift-update/"
  source="METR"
  date="24 Feb 2026"
>
Newer tools, bigger sample. The number moved because the tools moved. Hold every figure on this page that way.
</Evidence>

Returning developers: 18% *less* time. Confidence interval −38% to +9%. New recruits: 4% less time, interval −15% to +9%. Both intervals cross zero, so neither is a number you can bank.

Then METR called its own dataset an unreliable signal and started redesigning the method. Some developers refused to take part if it meant working without AI. Between 30% and 50% quietly skipped the tasks they least wanted to do by hand. METR reads its own estimate as a likely lower bound: the best AI use cases selected themselves out.

The honest sentence today: **the sign has probably flipped; the size is unknown.**

In May 2026 the same lab asked 349 technical workers what they *felt*. Median self-report: 1.4–2x more value, 3x more speed ([METR, 11 May 2026](https://metr.org/blog/2026-05-11-ai-usage-survey/)). METR attached its own warning, pointing back at the trial where people overestimated by 40 points.

<Callout type="tip" title="The pattern worth memorising">
Effects that get **measured** — stopwatch, control group, randomisation — come out small, sometimes negative. Effects people **report about themselves** start at 1.4x and climb. Nobody is lying. When a tool removes the boring part, you remember the flow, not the time spent re-reading a diff that was subtly wrong.
</Callout>

## Everyone uses it. Almost nobody trusts it.

Stack Overflow's 2025 developer survey, released 29 July 2025, collected more than 49,000 responses across 177 countries. 84% of developers use AI tools or plan to. 46% distrust the accuracy of the output, and only 3.1% trust it highly. The top frustration, named by 66%: an answer that is almost right. 45% say debugging AI-written code takes longer ([survey.stackoverflow.co](https://survey.stackoverflow.co/2025/ai)).

Not a contradiction. A profession has decided the tool is worth using *and* worth checking.

There is no 2026 edition of that survey yet. Plenty of articles run the 84% and the 3% under a "Stack Overflow 2026" headline. Those are the 2025 numbers wearing a new date. Check the year on every statistic anyone hands you, including the ones on this page.

Code quality points the same way. GitClear analysed 623 million code changes from 2023 to 2026: copy-pasted lines rose from 9.4% to 15.7% of new code in the first half of 2026, duplicated blocks rose about 81%, and refactoring fell from roughly 21% of changes in 2022 to 3.8% ([GitClear](https://www.gitclear.com/the_ai_code_quality_maintainability_gap)). GitClear sells code-quality tooling and the analysis is correlational, so take the direction, not the decimal: agents add, and rarely tidy unless you ask ([module 10](/en/learn/test-and-quality/)).

Security has not improved on its own. Veracode's July 2026 report found 56% of generated samples passed a security test, so 44% introduced a known kind of flaw — against 55% a year earlier ([Veracode](https://www.veracode.com/blog/2026-genai-code-security-report-ai-risk/)). Its March 2026 edition put syntax correctness above 95% ([Veracode](https://www.veracode.com/blog/spring-2026-genai-code-security/)). Models learned to write code that runs, not code that is safe. The test prompts carry no security instructions, so that is the default — and the default you can change ([module 11](/en/learn/security/)).

## The money says this is not a fad

- On 14 August 2026 SpaceX filed a [Form 8-K](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1181412/000162828026056945/spcx-20260814.htm): Anysphere, the company behind the Cursor editor, merged into a SpaceX subsidiary, all-stock, at an implied equity value of $60.0 billion. A code editor, absorbed by a rocket company.
- Lovable said in June 2026 it had reached $500 million in annualised revenue, a million new projects a week, over 50 million in total ([TechCrunch, 9 June 2026](https://techcrunch.com/2026/06/09/lovable-says-it-has-hit-500m-in-annualized-revenue-with-1-million-new-projects-a-week/)). Nobody outside the company can say how many are still alive.
- Anthropic said in February 2026 that Claude Code was running above a $2.5 billion annual run-rate, more than double where it began that year ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/news/anthropic-raises-30-billion-series-g-funding-380-billion-post-money-valuation)).
- JetBrains surveyed over 15,000 professional developers between May and July 2026: 90% use an AI coding agent at work at least weekly, 68% daily. Claude Code reached 39% work adoption, up from 18% in January 2026 ([JetBrains Research](https://blog.jetbrains.com/research/2026/08/ai-coding-agent-adoption-2026/)).

Argue about whether any one valuation is sane. You cannot argue that this is a niche.

**The floor rose.** What used to need a team, a budget and half a year now needs a weekend and a clear head. That is why you are reading this instead of a textbook.

**The ceiling did not move by itself.** Faros AI looked at telemetry from more than 10,000 developers on 1,255 teams: heavy AI users merged 98% more pull requests and spent 91% more time reviewing them. Pull requests grew 154% larger, bugs per developer rose 9%, and there was no measurable improvement at the company level ([Faros AI](https://www.faros.ai/blog/ai-software-engineering)). The work moved from writing to reading.

In March 2026 Mac Budkowski got a working prototype of a small Farcaster app in about an hour, then spent more than 100 hours making it shippable: interface, infrastructure, contract security, edge cases ([kanfa.macbudkowski.com](https://kanfa.macbudkowski.com/vibecoding-cryptosaurus)). The first hour is the floor. The hundred hours are the ceiling.

The *Science* study of 160,000 GitHub developers found the same shape: less experienced developers used AI on more of their work (37% versus 27%), and the measurable output gains went almost entirely to the experienced ones.

In January 2026 Anthropic put 52 mostly-junior engineers in front of an unfamiliar Python library. Those who delegated to AI scored 50% on a comprehension quiz afterwards; those who wrote it by hand scored 67%. The AI group finished two minutes faster, which is nothing. The ones who kept their scores asked the model to *explain* while it worked, not only to produce ([Anthropic Research](https://www.anthropic.com/research/AI-assistance-coding-skills)).

The floor is a gift; take it. The ceiling is a skill, and nobody hands it to you. Every module after this one is a technique for closing that gap.

Not replace. Redistribute, and the redistribution has an address. Stanford's Digital Economy Lab tracked US payroll data through June 2026. Employment of 22-to-25-year-olds in the most AI-exposed occupations runs about 19% below where it would be had it kept pace with less-exposed peers. Meanwhile 64% of developers in Stack Overflow's 2025 survey do not see AI as a threat to their job. The fastest-growing activity is review: reading code, judging it, being accountable for it. That job is harder to enter and more valuable to hold — an argument for learning to verify, not to prompt.

## Closing the gap starts with one prompt

When an agent finishes you see a confident summary, not the list of things it never checked. Ask for that list at the end of any session, before you accept anything.

<Prompt title="Close the gap" where="Claude Code · after the agent says it is done">
```text
Before I accept this work, answer these four questions. Do not change any code yet.

1. List every file you changed, one line each, saying what changed.
2. For each change, say how you verified it actually works.
   If you did not run it or test it, write "not verified".
3. List what you assumed about my code, my data or my environment
   but did not check.
4. Name the single change most likely to be wrong, and say why.
```
</Prompt>

Run it once today on anything you have already built with an agent. Question 2 is where the surprises live, and one honest "not verified" beats this whole page of statistics.

<ProjectStep n={2} title="Write down your prediction before you build anything">
